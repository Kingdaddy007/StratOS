# Feeling Curve and Pacing

Load when emotional or cognitive pacing, chapter weight, a remembered peak, inspection time, or ending impression materially affects the experience.

The purpose is not to make the site theatrical. It is to diagnose **where attention and understanding should rise, hold, pause, or resolve**.

Visual treatment remains the responsibility of Spatial Experience Design and motion owners.

## 1. Use feeling conditionally

Possible visitor states include:

- curiosity;
- orientation;
- desire;
- inspection;
- understanding;
- trust;
- surprise;
- confidence;
- calm;
- agency;
- readiness.

A visitor may need to understand rather than feel something.

Do not force an emotional label onto every chapter.

## 2. Diagnose pacing shape

Ask:

- Where should attention rise?
- Where should it hold?
- Where should the visitor slow for proof?
- Where does the sequence need orientation?
- Where is stillness useful?
- Is there a remembered peak?
- What should the ending leave clear or felt?

One primary peak is a useful discipline, not a law.

Some projects need:

- steady confidence;
- several modest peaks;
- one strong opening followed by quiet inspection;
- proof first, emotion later;
- a deliberately even editorial rhythm.

## 3. Separate narrative intensity from visual treatment

Storytelling can diagnose:

- this chapter is too intense too early;
- proof is rushed;
- the middle feels repetitive;
- visitors need more time to inspect;
- the ending does not resolve;
- inquiry arrives before readiness.

Storytelling should **not** prescribe the visual fix.

It may ask Spatial Experience Design / motion owners to explore:

- a quieter treatment;
- stronger emphasis;
- more inspection time;
- a clearer transition;
- reduced competition;
- a different visual hierarchy.

The visual owner decides whether that means image scale, whitespace, contrast, crop, typography, motion, or something else.

## 4. Inspection zones

Spatial work often needs moments where visitors can simply look, read, or compare.

Protect narrative space for:

- completed project imagery;
- proof-heavy captions;
- plans and drawings;
- before / after comparison;
- service clarity;
- inquiry information;
- material detail.

A cinematic experience that never allows inspection can weaken the work.

## 5. Lightweight intended curve

Use only when it improves the decision.

| Chapter | Intended state | Relative weight | Narrative reason |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| Opening | Curiosity | High | Establish point of view |
| Project proof | Inspection | Low | Let work establish authority |
| Design decision | Understanding | Medium | Reveal intelligence |
| Signature chapter | Recognition / surprise | High | Primary remembered moment |
| Practice | Trust | Low | Humanize and clarify |
| Inquiry | Readiness | Low | Resolve into action |

Do not copy this curve by default.

## 6. Cold-scroll review

After implementation or a representative prototype:

1. Review without implementation notes.
2. Record the visitor state each major chapter seems to create.
3. Mark where attention rises, holds, or drops.
4. Identify the perceived peak if there is one.
5. State the ending impression.
6. Compare intended vs felt.

Look for:

- filler;
- repeated intensity;
- long stretches with no new understanding;
- proof passed too quickly;
- prolonged suspense before basic orientation;
- accidental climax;
- weak ending;
- inquiry before readiness;
- motion that prevents inspection.

## 7. Recommend a narrative correction, not a visual recipe

Appropriate storytelling corrections include:

- move proof earlier;
- remove a redundant chapter;
- hold the project inspection beat longer;
- clarify what should be known before this section;
- reduce the number of competing narrative moments;
- change the order of chapters;
- make the ending resolve the earlier question;
- ask the visual owner to reduce or increase emphasis.

Do not prescribe image scale, contrast, whitespace, crop, motion amplitude, or other visual parameters.
