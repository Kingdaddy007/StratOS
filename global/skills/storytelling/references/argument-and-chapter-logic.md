# Argument and Chapter Logic

Load when a site, page, project, or important sequence needs a narrative route, when several plausible orders compete, or when chapters feel like reordered template sections.

Use this material to make a decision, not to produce a mandatory narrative worksheet.

## 1. Find the proposition only if it helps

Inspect:

- verified work;
- project decisions;
- audience questions;
- agreed brand premise;
- commercial reality;
- available proof.

A useful proposition may take this form:

> After seeing this evidence, visitors can understand [specific truth about the practice or work].

Use it to test selection, not as a mandatory headline.

Do not force one when:

- the task is a bounded section;
- the site is mainly browsable;
- a portfolio needs clear editorial framing more than one argument;
- several independent project stories carry the value;
- a proposition adds abstraction without changing a decision.

When no proposition is earned, record the live questions instead.

## 2. Select the narrative unit

Decide what actually needs a story:

- whole site;
- page / landing sequence;
- project case study;
- service explanation;
- section;
- browsable collection;
- another project-specific unit.

Do not force the whole site's argument into every section.

## 3. Compare genuinely different routes

Use these as questions, not a menu:

| Route question | May fit when | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lead with finished work? | Work is the strongest credible introduction | Beauty without context or authorship |
| Lead with a decision / transformation? | Constraint + response are documented and distinctive | Manufactured drama |
| Lead with point of view? | Belief is supported across real work | Assertion outruns proof |
| Lead with people / process? | Authorship or collaboration is central to trust | Biography delays the work |
| Lead with service clarity? | Visitors need fit / scope quickly | Utility crowds out character |
| Lead with a collection? | Range or contrast is itself evidence | Projects blur into one story |
| Lead with proof? | Trust burden is unusually high | Site becomes defensive |
| Lead another way? | Project material suggests a better form | Novelty without visitor benefit |

A conventional route may be correct.

An unconventional route must earn the orientation cost it creates.

## 4. Assign chapter jobs

For each proposed chapter answer:

1. What question or need brings the visitor here?
2. What changes in understanding?
3. What evidence makes that change credible?
4. What must already be understood?
5. What question or action becomes possible next?

Remove chapters that repeat the previous answer.

Combine claim and proof when separation weakens trust.

Split a chapter when the material genuinely needs more space for inspection or explanation.

## 5. Build dependencies

A lightweight dependency record can be:

| Chapter | Job | Evidence | Requires | Prepares |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening project | Establish point of view | Real completed work | Minimal orientation | Curiosity about decisions |
| Design decisions | Reveal intelligence | Detail, plan, captions | Result already seen | Trust in method |
| Broader work | Prove repeatability / range | Several projects | Point of view understood | Confidence |
| Practice | Explain authorship / collaboration | People + roles + process | Capability established | Inquiry |
| Inquiry | Next step | Service / contact clarity | Enough fit + trust | Action |

Do not copy this sequence by default.

## 6. Handle non-linear visits

When relevant, test:

### Directed visit
Visitor follows the intended order.

### Selective visit
Visitor jumps to Projects, Services, People, or Inquiry.

### Return visit
Visitor comes back for a remembered fact, project, service, or contact detail.

Each path should preserve enough context to avoid:

- project identity confusion;
- unsupported claims;
- unclear service scope;
- forced replay of cinematic sequences;
- loss of navigation / action.

Storytelling identifies context gaps. Spatial/UI owners decide the navigation model.

## 7. Project storytelling

A project may need more than a gallery when design intelligence matters.

Useful ingredients:

- starting condition;
- brief / context;
- constraint;
- key decision;
- alternatives or tradeoff;
- material relationship;
- spatial change;
- process;
- final result;
- verified outcome.

Not every project needs every ingredient.

Use the minimum explanation that makes the work more legible.

## 8. Detect narrative redundancy

Two chapters may be redundant when they:

- make the same claim;
- use the same proof;
- create the same visitor state;
- differ only in visual treatment;
- repeat the same service promise;
- restate the homepage thesis before new evidence appears.

Merge, cut, or assign one a different job.

## 9. Record consequential choices lightly

When a narrative choice matters, capture:

- selected route;
- strongest reason;
- proof burden;
- what it gives up;
- what would reopen the decision.

Use an existing brief or a few lines of notes. Do not create a narrative file merely to satisfy a format.
