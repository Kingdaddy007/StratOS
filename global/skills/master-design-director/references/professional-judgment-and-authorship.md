# Professional Judgment and Authorship

Load when the question is not merely “is this attractive?” but whether the work feels authored, resolved, specific, credible, or dependent on generic prestige signals.

## 1. Separate preference, convention, and failure

A critic may personally dislike a choice that still works.

Label the issue appropriately:

- **Preference** — another solution may be more appealing, but the current one is defensible.
- **Convention** — a familiar pattern is being used; familiarity alone is not failure.
- **Risk** — the choice may fail under certain content, viewport, asset, or visitor conditions.
- **Failure** — available evidence shows the design is not doing its intended job.

Do not present preference as objective law.

## 2. Authorship test

Ask what appears generated by this project's material.

Possible sources of authorship:

- subject relationship;
- distinctive imagery;
- content hierarchy;
- project sequence;
- unusual proof relationship;
- typography tied to content;
- material / spatial behaviour;
- brand personality;
- purposeful interaction;
- tension or contrast;
- a repeated rule that belongs to the work.

A familiar grid may still be authored.

A custom shader may still be generic.

## 3. Removability test

Mentally remove:

- fashionable font;
- luxury colour palette;
- awards-style motion;
- heavy 3D;
- oversized type;
- admired-reference surface styling.

What project-specific logic remains?

If almost nothing remains, the work may be polished but weakly authored.

Do not require every project to pass by being visually unprecedented. The question is specificity, not novelty.

## 4. Prestige-signal audit

Common status signals include:

- beige / mineral palette;
- thin serif;
- huge whitespace;
- tiny uppercase labels;
- slow easing;
- cinematic grain;
- dark editorial backgrounds;
- luxury photography;
- large display type;
- sparse navigation;
- WebGL / 3D.

None is inherently good or bad.

Ask whether each serves:

- brand character;
- subject;
- hierarchy;
- visitor;
- content;
- project constraints.

Do not call something “false luxury” merely because it uses a familiar luxury signal. Call it unsupported when the signal is doing more work than the brand / content.

## 5. AI-like polish

Work can feel AI-generated when it has:

- attractive but non-specific composition;
- perfectly balanced generic sections;
- repeated “premium” motifs;
- effects without content pressure;
- plausible but invented proof;
- inconsistent details hidden by overall polish;
- too many fashionable ingredients with no governing relationship.

Do not diagnose “AI-generated” from aesthetic style alone.

Instead name the concrete sameness or inconsistency.

## 6. Almost-good-but-unresolved

Common signs:

- hierarchy works broadly but one competing element breaks the eye path;
- typography is attractive but wraps badly at real viewport sizes;
- strong hero has no downstream grammar;
- image treatment is elegant but project identity becomes unclear;
- motion feels good forward but poor on reverse / interruption;
- spacing is mathematically consistent but optically wrong;
- multiple good ideas compete rather than form one system.

Senior judgment often means finding the small structural conflict that prevents good work from feeling resolved.

## 7. Optical sensitivity

Use systems as baselines, then inspect perception.

Examples:

- mathematically centered type can appear visually low / high;
- equal padding can feel unequal around different letterforms / images;
- same font size can have different visual weight;
- aligned boxes may still create an uneven rhythm;
- identical gaps may not produce identical perceived spacing.

Do not turn optical judgment into arbitrary nudging. Explain what relationship is being corrected.

## 8. Distinctiveness

Ask:

- Would this still belong to this project if the logo disappeared?
- Which choice would be difficult to justify for an unrelated client?
- Is the memorable moment tied to real content?
- Does the site have one or two relationships visitors may remember?
- Is the identity coming from structure, or only styling?

Distinctiveness is contextual.

Do not optimize for “never seen before.”

## 9. Confidence

State confidence based on evidence.

A single screenshot can support composition critique.

It usually cannot prove:

- scroll pacing;
- motion quality;
- loading;
- responsive behaviour;
- full-site continuity.

Keep critique inside the evidence actually available.

## 10. Practical relationship tests

Use these as quick perception checks when a composition is attractive but difficult to diagnose.

### Blur / shrink test
Blur the page mentally or reduce it to a small thumbnail.

Ask:
- Which visual mass wins?
- Is that the intended first read?
- Does the hierarchy survive without detail?

### First / second / third read
Trace where the eye goes in sequence.

Ask:
- Where does attention split?
- Where does the eye stall?
- Is the second read supporting the first or competing with it?

### Remove surface styling mentally
Imagine the design without:
- colour;
- beautiful imagery;
- fashionable type;
- motion polish.

Ask whether the underlying composition and relationships still work.

### Opening-to-later-section test
Compare the first encounter with a later representative section.

Ask:
- Which rules recur?
- Which rules develop?
- Which rules intentionally break?
- Does the concept still exist after the hero?

### Logo-swap test
Imagine replacing the logo with an unrelated organisation.

Ask:
- Which choices still feel specific to this project?
- Which choices could transfer almost unchanged?

These are diagnostic exercises, not automatic verdicts. Use them to reveal the relationship that needs attention.
