# Critique Prioritization and Teaching

Load when many critique notes compete, when the designer asks for direct pushback, or when the critique should build transferable judgment.

## 1. Rank by consequence

Use levels rather than a flat note list:

### Level 1 — Truth / strategy conflict
The design implies something unsupported or contradicts the project.

### Level 2 — Concept / experience conflict
The governing idea is unclear, detached from the subject, or cannot continue.

### Level 3 — Composition / hierarchy conflict
The idea is viable but attention and relationships are poorly organized.

### Level 4 — System / execution conflict
Typography, imagery, motion, spacing, or responsive behaviour breaks the system.

### Level 5 — Polish
Small refinements once higher-level relationships work.

Do not fix Level 1–2 problems with Level 5 work.

## 2. Highest-leverage critique

A useful critique should identify:

- the issue that changes the most downstream;
- the cheapest test that can confirm it;
- which existing strength should be protected.

Avoid giving 30 equal-weight notes.

## 3. Subtract, replace, add, or leave

Every critique recommendation can be framed as:

- **Subtract** — remove a competing / unsupported element.
- **Replace** — swap an operation while preserving the job.
- **Add** — supply missing hierarchy, evidence, contrast, context, or behaviour.
- **Leave** — explicitly protect a working decision from unnecessary iteration.

“Leave” is important. Continuous redesign can damage coherent work.

## 4. Pushback without performance

When the user asks for:
- “make it pop”;
- “make it more premium”;
- “add more animation”;
- “fill the empty space”;
- “make it Awwwards”;

translate the request into the likely design problem.

For example:

- “make it pop” may mean hierarchy is weak;
- “premium” may mean image quality / specificity / finish is poor;
- “more animation” may mean pacing is flat;
- “fill space” may mean grouping or proportion feels unresolved.

Do not reject the request theatrically. Diagnose it.

## 5. Teach the reusable principle

When helpful, explain:

- **What you are seeing**
- **What the critic is checking**
- **Why it matters**
- **What transfers to other projects**

Keep this short unless the user asks to learn deeply.

## 6. Use a decision challenge selectively

A useful challenge may be:

> If we remove this effect, what project-specific idea disappears?

or:

> What must visitors notice here before they scroll?

or:

> Does this choice protect the room or compete with it?

Do not force the user to answer before reversible work can continue.

The challenge is for clarity, not permission.

## 7. Critique alternatives

When proposing a correction, give enough contrast to expose the principle.

Example:

- Current: every section fades and rises at equal intensity.
- Option A: keep the gesture only at chapter starts.
- Option B: reserve movement for image / proof handoffs and let reading sections hold.
- Principle: repetition should teach grammar without flattening hierarchy.

Do not generate full alternative concepts unless that is the actual request.

## 8. Critique language

Prefer concrete language:

Weak:
- “This doesn't feel premium.”
- “It needs more sophistication.”
- “The spacing is off.”

Stronger:
- “The project title and CTA have nearly equal visual weight, so the eye has no clear first read.”
- “The hero image is strong, but the later grid abandons the crop and type relationships that made the opening specific.”
- “The top margin is numerically consistent with the system, but the large cap-height makes the heading appear crowded against the frame.”

## 9. Uncertainty

Say when evidence is insufficient.

Examples:

- screenshot cannot prove scroll pacing;
- desktop mockup cannot prove mobile;
- prototype with placeholder imagery cannot prove crop quality;
- animation description cannot prove felt timing.

Then name the smallest evidence needed.

## 10. Prototype verdicts

For a costly prototype, recommendations may be:

- **Expand** — core assumption is supported.
- **Revise** — concept remains viable but a material relationship needs change.
- **Simplify** — value survives with lower complexity.
- **Return to concept** — the tested assumption undermines the direction.

These are recommendations to the project owner, not authority commands.

## 11. Cold read before rationale

For a sufficiently developed prototype or site, experience it first as a visitor before reading the design rationale.

Ask:

- What is this?
- What deserves attention first?
- What appears to be evidence or real work?
- What can I do next?
- What survives after the first screen?

Then compare the observed experience with the intended direction.

Use the **riskiest assumption wherever it lies** as the main review target. The hero is not automatically the most important test.

If a specific missing state could reverse the judgment, inspect that state rather than expanding the critique.

## 12. Decision outcome vocabulary

For a consequential review, it can help to distinguish the type of response the work needs:

- **Protect** — the decision is working and further change would likely weaken it.
- **Refine** — the direction is sound; composition, crop, copy, timing, or execution needs adjustment.
- **Simplify** — the core value survives with less complexity or lower visitor / production burden.
- **Reopen** — the governing concept conflicts with project truth, visitor purpose, subject integrity, or available material.
- **Inspect further** — one specific missing state, asset, or condition could reverse the judgment.

Use these as recommendation labels, not approval authority.

They complement, rather than replace, the action vocabulary:
**Subtract / Replace / Add / Leave**.
