# Section Invention and Composition

Load when one section feels generic, the designer does not know what should happen next, or an important chapter needs a stronger visual idea.

This reference helps translate **section meaning and material into composition** without requiring every section to become a spectacle or literal metaphor.

## 1. Start with the section job

State the section's job in one sentence.

Examples:

- establish authorship;
- let visitors inspect project quality;
- explain a design decision;
- show how the team works;
- communicate process;
- compare before / after;
- create trust before inquiry;
- transition from atmosphere into proof.

Do not begin with an effect.

Also do not forbid an effect from being the initial spark. If a visual idea came first, temporarily keep it and test whether it can serve the section job.

## 2. Inventory the real material

What actually exists?

- one strong image;
- several related images;
- portraits;
- names / roles;
- process steps;
- project labels;
- before / after;
- a material detail;
- a quote;
- plan / drawing;
- numbers;
- video;
- 3D model;
- little or no visual material.

The material is part of the creative constraint.

## 3. Find a generative lead

Useful leads include:

- **relationship** — founder/team, object/room, detail/whole, client/process;
- **hierarchy** — one dominant subject with supporting elements;
- **contrast** — old/new, dark/light, raw/refined, close/wide;
- **sequence** — reveal, assemble, inspect, compare, resolve;
- **geometry** — grid, circle, axis, curve, corridor, repeated opening;
- **rhythm** — alternating image sizes, stagger, cadence, repetition;
- **material** — texture, translucency, reflection, weight, softness;
- **personality** — precise, theatrical, playful, restrained, tactile;
- **absence** — one image held long enough to matter; deliberate stillness.

The lead is not automatically the concept. It is a constraint for invention.

## 4. Build the composition before the motion

Ask:

- What is dominant?
- What is secondary?
- What enters first?
- What must be visible simultaneously?
- What can overlap?
- What needs breathing room?
- Where can type live without hiding proof?
- Is the composition stable without animation?
- What changes at mobile width?

Possible composition moves are vocabulary, not templates:

- single dominant plate;
- asymmetric editorial split;
- layered foreground / background;
- central nucleus + orbiting/supporting elements;
- sequential full-viewport chapters;
- comparative pair;
- expanding detail-to-wide relationship;
- strip / procession;
- anchored type + traveling imagery;
- spatial frame / threshold;
- deliberate hard cut between incompatible worlds.

Use none if another structure fits better.

## 5. Decide whether movement improves the idea

Movement can serve:

- hierarchy;
- reveal;
- continuity;
- physicality;
- transformation;
- rhythm;
- atmosphere;
- attention;
- relationship;
- interaction feedback.

Ask:

- What changes in the visitor's understanding because this moves?
- Could framing, crop, type, light, or sequence create the result more simply?
- Does the source support the movement honestly?
- Should movement be scroll-controlled, autonomous, interactive, pre-rendered, or absent?
- What is the reduced-motion equivalent?

If movement does not improve the section, keep it still.

## 6. Inherit or break the site grammar deliberately

A section may:

- **inherit** the established crop, transition, type, camera direction, or motion physics;
- **intensify** one existing rule for emphasis;
- **pause** the grammar to let proof breathe;
- **break** the grammar for a justified turning point.

Do not invent a new visual language for every section.

## 7. Mini section packet

When the idea is consequential, capture:

- **job** — what this chapter must accomplish;
- **material** — what real content/assets exist;
- **generative lead** — what organizes the composition;
- **composition** — dominant / secondary / overlap / type relationship;
- **behaviour** — still or moving, and why;
- **continuity** — how it belongs to the wider site;
- **mobile** — what changes;
- **risk** — what could make it fail.

A few sentences or a sketch may be enough.

## 8. Example: founder + team

This is an example of reasoning, not a reusable formula.

- **Job:** communicate leadership and coordinated practice.
- **Material:** founder portrait, team portraits, names, roles, copy about collaboration.
- **Generative lead:** hierarchy + relationship.
- **Composition:** founder as nucleus; supporting team emerges around that center.
- **Behaviour:** staggered expansion / orbit can make the organizational relationship felt.
- **Continuity:** reuse the site's existing motion weight and typography behaviour rather than inventing a separate visual world.
- **Risk:** if the spiral becomes spectacle, hides names, or implies status relationships the studio does not claim, simplify it.

Another team may need a completely different solution.

## 9. Diagnose generic sections

A section often feels generic because:

- its composition could belong to any brand;
- image and copy have no meaningful relationship;
- it repeats the previous section's hierarchy without purpose;
- motion is added after the fact;
- the strongest material is presented too small;
- too many elements compete equally;
- the section ignores a relationship or contrast already present in the content;
- it breaks the site's grammar accidentally;
- it copies an admired reference without the source conditions that made it work.

Correct the **composition or content relationship** before adding more effects.

## 10. Smallest useful test

Prototype only what could change the decision:

- hierarchy at actual viewport size;
- portrait overlap;
- type-safe area;
- before/after readability;
- orbit / spiral spacing;
- mobile stacking;
- crop continuity;
- first and last motion states;
- whether the section still works when motion is removed.

The goal is not to prove the section is impressive.

The goal is to learn whether the idea is true, legible, usable, and worth expanding.
